## 1. Writing as Thinking: The Core Argument

The most important theoretical framework for understanding why AI text generation
doesn't make typing obsolete is the distinction between **knowledge telling** and
**knowledge transforming** (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 1987).

### Knowledge Telling vs. Knowledge Transforming

- **Knowledge telling**: The writer retrieves content from memory using topic and
  genre cues and writes it down with minimal transformation. Content flows from
  memory to page with little restructuring.

- **Knowledge transforming**: The writer engages in a dialectical process between
  *content space* (what they know) and *rhetorical space* (how to communicate it).
  The interaction between these two problem spaces generates new understanding. The
  writer literally thinks thoughts they would not have thought without writing.

**Note on AI**: Modern AI systems clearly perform something resembling knowledge
transformation in their output: synthesizing novel connections, restructuring
understanding for audiences, generating insights absent from any single source.
The framework's relevance is not about whether AI can produce transformed output
(it plainly can) but about what happens *in the human writer's mind* during
composition. When you write, you reorganize your own understanding as a
consequence of the struggle to articulate. When AI writes for you, the output may
be excellent, but you do not undergo that cognitive transformation yourself.

### Supporting Evidence

- **Emig (1977)**: Writing is unique among language processes because it is both
  productive and immediately available for review. You read what you wrote, creating
  a feedback loop that doesn't exist in speech.

- **Vygotsky (1934/1986)**: Written language requires the writer to create the
  context a listener would normally provide, forcing more explicit and structured
  thinking than speech.

- **Langer & Applebee (1987)**: Large empirical study finding analytic writing led
  to better content learning than reading alone. Different types of writing engaged
  different types of thinking.

- **Galbraith (1999, 2009)**: Proposed a dual-process model where "knowledge
  constituting" writing generates new understanding through text production itself.
  Low-outline "discovery" writers gained more new ideas through writing than
  high-outline planners, because the writing process generates connections not
  available through planning alone.

- **Graham & Hebert (2011)**: Meta-analysis showing writing about texts improves
  reading comprehension -- the act of writing forces organization, integration, and
  reflection that reading alone does not.

- **Kellogg (2008)**: Extended Bereiter & Scardamalia's framework to three stages:
  knowledge telling (novice), knowledge transforming (intermediate), knowledge
  crafting (expert). Argues it takes 10+ years of deliberate practice to reach
  knowledge crafting.

## 2. Cognitive Differences: Writing vs. Speech

Writing and speaking are fundamentally different cognitive operations, not just
different output channels.

### Key Distinctions

| Dimension | Speaking | Writing/Typing |
|-----------|----------|----------------|
| Planning | Incremental, real-time | Recursive, asynchronous |
| Revision | Cannot unsay; can only repair | Can delete, reorder, restructure |
| Memory load | Must hold plan in working memory | Text persists as external memory |
| Social context | Listener present; co-constructed | Writer alone; must construct context |
| Working memory | Phonological loop dominant | All three WM components engaged |
| Register | Informal, redundant | Formal, compressed |

### Research Base

- **Levelt (1989)**: Foundational model of speech production -- incremental,
  pipelined, with limited conscious revision.

- **Flower & Hayes (1981)**: Foundational cognitive model of writing. Three
  recursive processes (planning, translating, reviewing) that constantly interrupt
  each other. Critical insight: writing is not linear but recursive.

- **Bourdin & Fayol (1994)**: Writing taxes working memory more than speaking at
  the transcription level, but the persistence of the written trace offloads memory
  for text-already-produced, enabling more complex composition.

- **Kellogg (2007)**: Writing recruits visual-spatial working memory components that
  speaking does not, because writers must manage the spatial layout of text and
  reread their output.

- **Chenoweth & Hayes (2001)**: Writing allows recursive revision, decoupling
  production from social interaction, freeing cognitive resources for planning and
  restructuring.

## 3. The Neuroscience of Typing

### Brain Regions

- **Typing** activates: left superior parietal lobule, left premotor cortex,
  bilateral SMA, cerebellum, and bilateral premotor regions for bimanual
  coordination (Purcell et al., 2011; Planton et al., 2013).

- **Handwriting** activates: contralateral primary motor cortex, superior parietal
  lobule, and uniquely recruits Exner's area with letter-specific motor traces
  (Longcamp et al., 2003).

- **Speaking** activates: Broca's area, primary motor cortex (mouth/larynx), SMA,
  superior temporal gyrus (auditory feedback), insula, basal ganglia (Perrone-
  Bertolotti et al., 2014).

- **Shared**: Both typing and handwriting activate left inferior frontal gyrus and
  left fusiform for orthographic processing (Planton et al., 2013).

### Expert vs. Novice Typing

- **Novel motor sequences** (hunt-and-peck) activate prefrontal and parietal
  regions heavily. **Well-learned sequences** (touch typing) shift to SMA and
  basal ganglia -- subcortical, automatic control (Segal & Petrides, 2012).

- **Ihara et al. (2021)**: Even imagining typing specific words activates
  finger-specific motor cortex, demonstrating that typing does create embodied
  representations organized as finger-key mappings.

### Critical Gap in Literature

Most neuroimaging studies comparing "typing" with handwriting use novice or
hunt-and-peck typists. Very few studies examine expert touch typists (60+ WPM).
The "handwriting advantage" in brain activation may largely reflect a comparison
between skilled handwriters and unskilled typists, not an inherent modality
difference.

## 4. Input Modality and Learning

### Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) -- "The Pen Is Mightier Than the Keyboard"

The most widely cited study in this space. Laptop note-takers transcribed more but
processed less. Longhand note-takers, constrained by speed, were forced to
summarize and rephrase, leading to deeper conceptual understanding.

**However**: Subsequent replications have been mixed:
- **Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson (2019)**: Failed to replicate the learning
  advantage in a pre-registered study.
- **Urry et al. (2021)**: Large-scale registered replication (N=1,400+) found no
  significant advantage for longhand.

**Resolution**: The effect appears to be about **processing depth** (forced
paraphrasing due to slower speed) rather than **modality per se**. Once typing
becomes automatic and the typist actively composes rather than transcribes, the
modality difference diminishes.

### van der Meer & van der Weel (2017, 2021)

EEG studies showing handwriting produced more theta/alpha oscillations (associated
with memory encoding) than typing. But critically, "typing" in these studies was
single-finger or hunt-and-peck, not expert touch typing.

### Key Takeaway

The input modality effects on learning are **moderated by skill level**. The
research does not show that typing is cognitively inferior to handwriting -- it
shows that *unskilled* typing is cognitively inferior. Expert typing that enables
active composition may be cognitively equivalent or superior for sustained text
production.

## 5. Expert Typing: Automaticity and Flow

### The Two-Loop Model

**Logan & Crump (2011)** proposed that skilled typing has two loops:
- **Outer loop**: Language and word-level planning (conscious)
- **Inner loop**: Keystroke execution (automatic)

In expert typists, the inner loop is fully automatic -- typists cannot report which
fingers they use or where keys are located. This frees the outer loop entirely for
composition.

### Evidence for Automaticity

- **Salthouse (1986)**: Expert typists process text 3-8 characters ahead of the
  current keystroke ("eye-hand span"). Speed gains come from chunking and
  anticipation, not faster fingers.

- **Yamaguchi & Logan (2014)**: Skilled typists organize keystrokes into word-level
  chunks. Disrupting chunking dramatically slows typing.

- **Rieger (2015)**: Expert typists show no dual-task cost when typing familiar
  words while performing a concurrent cognitive task -- near-zero cognitive demand
  for keystroke execution.

### Typing and Flow States

- **Csikszentmihalyi (1990)**: Flow requires skill-challenge balance. For writing,
  the motor act must be automatic so it doesn't interrupt composition.

- **Chenoweth & Hayes (2001)**: Faster typists produced longer uninterrupted bursts
  of text ("writing bursts"), consistent with sustained flow.

- **Galbraith (2009)**: "Knowledge-constituting" writing requires sustained,
  uninterrupted text production -- the writer must externalize thought at the speed
  of ideation, which requires fast, automatic typing.

### The Fluency Paradox

- **McCutchen (1996)**: Transcription fluency is a critical bottleneck. When typing
  is effortful, it consumes working memory needed for higher-order composition.

- **Alves, Castro & Olive (2008)**: Faster typists distribute pauses at clause and
  sentence boundaries (for planning); slow typists pause mid-word (for
  transcription). Typing fluency shifts cognitive bottlenecks upward.

- **Grabowski (2008)**: Even among university students, typing skill varies
  enormously and correlates with writing behavior.

**The paradox**: Too little fluency starves higher-order processes. But
transcription that is too easy (dictation, or AI generating text) may bypass the
knowledge-transforming processes that make writing cognitively valuable. The sweet
spot is where transcription is automatic enough not to interfere, but the writer is
still doing the generative cognitive work.

## 6. AI's Impact on Writing Workflows

### Productivity Effects

- **Noy & Zhang (2023)** (*Science*): ChatGPT reduced writing time by 40% and
  improved quality ratings, with the largest effect for lower-skilled writers.
  This early study suggested AI primarily raised the floor.

- **Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2023)** (NBER): AI assistance increased customer
  support productivity by 14% on average, with 34% gains for novice workers.

**Important caveat (2026 update)**: These early studies used 2023-era models on
routine tasks. By 2026, with frontier models (Claude Opus 4.5/4.6, GPT-5.2,
Gemini 3), AI demonstrably raises the ceiling too. Solo developers using agentic
coding tools like Claude Code are doing the work of entire teams -- not by
lowering the bar, but by amplifying expert capability. The "floor not ceiling"
finding was an artifact of early, weaker models and constrained study designs.
The correct framing is: AI raises the floor for novices AND multiplies the
throughput of experts, but through different mechanisms (quality improvement for
novices, velocity and scope multiplication for experts).

### Cognitive Shift: Generation to Evaluation

- **Lee, Liang & Yang (2022)** (*CHI*): AI suggestions shifted writers from
  generation to evaluation/selection mode. Writers spent more time choosing between
  options and less time generating from scratch.

- **Ippolito, Yuan, Coenen & Burnam (2022)**: Professional writers reported AI
  suggestions sometimes derailed their train of thought and pushed them toward more
  generic text.

- **Doshi & Hauser (2024)** (*Science Advances*): AI-assisted stories were rated
  higher individually, but the overall corpus was more homogeneous. AI boosts
  individual quality at the cost of collective diversity.

### The "Last Mile" Problem

- **Lee et al. (2022)**: Human writers made substantive edits to 70%+ of AI
  suggestions -- factual corrections, tone adjustments, logical restructuring,
  voice consistency. The last mile of editing is cognitively demanding and requires
  typing.

- **Bhat, Mehta & Yun (2023)** (*CHI*): Longer AI suggestions reduced keystrokes
  but increased cognitive load. Users who accepted long suggestions without editing
  produced lower-quality text.

- AI consistently struggles with: domain-specific accuracy, consistent voice,
  contextual appropriateness, logical coherence in complex arguments, and ethical
  judgment. All require human intervention via keyboard.

## 7. The Deskilling Risk

### Theoretical Foundation

- **Bainbridge (1983)** -- "Ironies of Automation": The more reliable the
  automation, the less practice the human gets, and the less competent they become
  at the task they must perform when automation fails.

- **Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens (2000)**: Higher automation leads to
  "complacency" and "out-of-the-loop" effects. Operators lose situational
  awareness.

- **Risko & Gilbert (2016)**: Cognitive offloading -- people reallocate rather than
  lose capacity, but the offloaded skill may weaken if not exercised. Writing is
  identified as a "generative" task where offloading carries higher risk.

### Empirical Evidence

- **Dell'Acqua, McFowland, Mollick et al. (2023)** (Harvard/BCG): AI improved
  performance on tasks inside the AI's capability "frontier" by 40%, but
  **decreased** performance on tasks outside the frontier by 19% when consultants
  over-relied on AI. The strongest evidence for "falling asleep at the wheel."

- **Darvishi et al. (2024)**: Students who relied heavily on AI-generated text
  showed weaker performance on unassisted writing assessments, especially for
  argumentative and analytical writing.

- **Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011)** (*Science*): People recall less when they know
  information is digitally accessible ("Google effects on memory"). Foundational
  for concerns about AI cognitive offloading.

- **Carr (2010, 2014)**: Argued automation of cognitive tasks leads to deskilling
  when the human is relegated to a supervisory role.

### The Bereiter & Scardamalia Connection

If AI handles the knowledge-telling work, humans may lose the capacity for
knowledge transformation -- not because the skill was automated, but because the
practice that develops it was eliminated. Knowledge transforming is not innate; it
requires years of deliberate writing practice (Kellogg, 2008: 10+ years).

## 8. AI and Programming

### The State of Play (Early 2026)

The landscape has shifted dramatically since the early Copilot studies. As of 2026:

- **92% of US developers** use AI coding tools daily (Second Talent, 2026)
- **46% of new code** is AI-generated (GitClear analysis of 211M changed lines)
- **Both Microsoft and Google** report ~25% of their code is AI-generated
- Anthropic reports ~90% of the code for Claude Code is written by Claude Code
- Andrej Karpathy coined **"vibe coding"** in early 2025 -- accepting AI-generated
  code without reviewing it, relying on results and follow-up prompts

### The METR Study: The Counterpoint That Matters

**METR (July 2025)**: Randomized controlled trial with 16 experienced open-source
developers, 246 issues, using Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet.

- Developers took **19% longer** with AI tools than without
- Developers **predicted** AI would make them 24% faster
- Even after the slowdown, they **believed** AI had sped them up by 20%
- Less than 44% of AI generations were accepted; rest was wasted review time

**METR Update (Feb 2026)**: Expanded to 57 developers, 800+ tasks, 143 repos.
Results softened (-4% for new developers vs. -18% for returning ones) but were
confounded by severe selection bias: 30-50% of developers **refused to submit
tasks they didn't want to do without AI**, and many declined to participate at all
without AI access. METR acknowledged their estimates likely underrepresent actual
AI productivity gains.

**Key insight**: The METR study is not evidence that AI doesn't help. It is
evidence that AI's benefits are hardest to measure in the exact context where
typing skill matters most -- experienced developers working on complex, familiar
codebases. For boilerplate and greenfield work, the productivity gains are real.

### Code Quality Concerns

- **GitClear (2025)**: Analysis of 211M changed lines found code churn (new code
  revised within 2 weeks) rose from 3.1% (2020) to 5.7% (2024). Refactoring
  dropped from 25% to <10% of changes. Copy-pasted code rose from 8.3% to 12.3%.
  Code duplication blocks rose eightfold.

- **CodeRabbit (Dec 2025)**: Analysis of 470 open-source PRs found AI co-authored
  code had ~1.7x more "major" issues, 75% more misconfigurations, and 2.74x
  higher security vulnerability rates.

- **44% of developers** observe declining fundamental programming skills among
  junior developers (Second Talent, 2026)

### The "Typing Is Dead" Voices

Experienced engineers are publicly reporting changed relationships with typing:
- Thorsten Ball: "typing out code by hand now frustrates me"
- Adam Wathan: "any time I have to type precise syntax" feels like "a tedious chore"
- Peter Steinberger: doesn't "read much code anymore" from AI systems

These are real signals. But they describe a shift in *what* is typed, not the
elimination of typing. Prompting, reviewing, editing, and directing AI all require
keyboard input -- often more precise and cognitively demanding than the boilerplate
code they replace.

### What Changes

- Typing shifts from boilerplate to higher-level direction and prompting
- Developers type "what I want" instead of "how to do it"
- More time reviewing, editing, and rejecting AI output
- Total keystrokes may decrease, but per-keystroke cognitive value increases
- "Vibe coding" works for prototypes; production code still needs human judgment

### What Doesn't Change

- Manual typing essential for: specifying intent precisely, editing/correcting AI
  output, novel or underdocumented domains, security-critical code
- IDEs remain keyboard-driven
- No meaningful shift toward voice-based coding despite AI adoption
- The developers who refused to work without AI in the METR study still need
  keyboards to interact with AI

## 9. Why Voice Won't Replace Keyboards

### Fundamental Limitations

- **Shneiderman (2000)**: Speech limited for spatial manipulation, precise editing,
  and situations requiring silence. Predicted keyboards would remain dominant for
  professional text work. Still correct 25+ years later.

- **Karat et al. (1999)**: Error correction in speech consumed ~75% of dictation
  time. Net throughput was lower than typing for anything requiring revision.

- **Ruan et al. (2018)**: Speech was ~3x faster than phone keyboards for short
  messages, but users still preferred typing in most real-world contexts.

### Environmental and Social Barriers

- 60-70% of knowledge workers are in open/semi-open offices (Steelcase/Gensler
  surveys)
- Voice input infeasible for confidential content (HIPAA, legal privilege)
- Social acceptability of talking to devices remains low in professional settings
- Libraries, courtrooms, shared living spaces, late-night work

### The Editing Problem

- **Leijten & Van Waes (2013)**: Revision constitutes 40-60% of total keystrokes
  for professional writers. Voice handles first-draft dictation but fails at
  revision.
- Text editing is fundamentally a spatial/visual task poorly served by serial voice
  commands.

### Accessibility

- ~466 million people worldwide have disabling hearing loss (WHO, 2021)
- ~7.5% of global population has some communication disorder
- Keyboard input is not a preference but a necessity for these populations
- W3C WCAG 2.1 requires keyboard accessibility as baseline

## 10. The Ergonomic Keyboard Market Is Growing

- **Grand View Research (2023)**: Ergonomic keyboard market projected to grow at
  ~7-8% CAGR through 2030
- **Fortune Business Insights (2023)**: Mechanical keyboard market ~$1.9B in 2022,
  projected ~$3.5B by 2030
- Post-COVID remote work drove measurable spike in ergonomic peripheral sales
- Ergonomic keyboards are the fastest-growing keyboard subsegment
- Health-driven demand is orthogonal to AI-driven typing reduction

**Key insight**: The ergonomic keyboard market is insulated because its core value
proposition (RSI prevention/management) persists regardless of whether total typing
volume declines. Even reduced typing still causes injury on poorly designed
hardware.

## 11. Pain Destroys the Cognitive Benefits of Typing

This section bridges the cognitive science with the ergonomic argument.

### The Mechanism

1. Typing automaticity enables flow states and frees working memory for composition
2. RSI/pain disrupts automaticity by demanding conscious attention
3. Disrupted automaticity consumes cognitive resources meant for composition
4. The writer loses both the physical and cognitive benefits of skilled typing

### Evidence

- **Eccleston & Crombez (1999)**: Pain inherently captures attention, disrupting
  ongoing cognitive tasks. For typists with RSI, pain directly competes with
  composition processes.

- **Rempel et al. (2006)**: Computer users with upper extremity pain showed
  decreased typing speed and increased errors.

- **Baker & Cidboy (2006)**: Even without diagnosed RSI, prolonged typing sessions
  produced measurable increases in muscle tension and accuracy decreases.

- **Feuerstein et al. (1997)**: Higher keystroke force associated with greater
  symptoms. Workers who developed symptoms typed with 2-4x necessary force.

- **Marcus et al. (2002)**: Annual incidence of hand/arm symptoms: 21%. Once
  symptoms appeared, typing speed decreased 15-30%.

- **Moore et al. (2019)**: Workers with RSI reported not just physical but
  *cognitive* disruption: difficulty concentrating, loss of creative momentum,
  anxiety about flare-ups.

### The Ergonomic Keyboard Argument

If typing is a cognitive tool (not just an output channel), then anything that
disrupts typing disrupts thinking. Ergonomic keyboards that prevent pain are not
just physical health devices -- they are cognitive performance tools. The DataHand
evidence (71% pain reduction, Harrington study) and Svalboard's improvements on
that design directly support sustained cognitive performance.

## 12. Case Study: The Super-Powered Developer

Svalboard project itself is a living case study of AI-augmented knowledge work
-- and it demonstrates exactly why typing becomes *more* critical, not less, as AI
multiplies human capability.

### Three Roles, Three AI Workflows, One Keyboard

**The Developer: Ira Cooper (ILC)**

Ira Cooper is a solo firmware and software developer who, with a Claude Code Max
subscription, is doing the work of what would traditionally require a large
development team. His contributions to the Svalboard ecosystem span keyboard
firmware (QMK/Vial), web-based configuration tools, protocol implementations, and
systems integration -- a scope that would normally demand specialists across
embedded systems, web development, protocol design, and DevOps.

This is the "super-powered developer" pattern emerging in 2025-2026: not a junior
dev getting AI-assisted autocomplete, but an experienced engineer using agentic AI
as a force multiplier across an entire product stack. The AI handles boilerplate,
generates implementations from architectural direction, runs tests, and iterates on
code -- but every cycle requires the developer to type: prompts specifying intent,
architectural decisions, code reviews, corrections, and the precise edits that turn
generated code into production-quality software.

The METR study's finding that experienced developers were 19% slower with AI makes
a specific kind of sense here: on *small, well-scoped issues in a familiar
codebase*, the overhead of reviewing AI suggestions can exceed the time saved. But
that framing misses the point. The super-powered developer isn't using AI to do
the same tasks faster. They're using AI to do *ten times more tasks* -- to operate
across an entire product surface that would be impossible for a single person
without AI. The typing doesn't decrease; it *changes character*, from
implementation to direction.

**The UX Designer: Bernard Kerr**

Bernard Kerr, a UX designer, uses AI tools for rapid prototyping and design
iteration on keybard-ng, the Svalboard's custom web-based layout editor. His
workflow represents another facet of AI augmentation: a designer who can now
prototype functional interfaces directly, without waiting for developer cycles.
The AI generates implementations from design intent, but the intent must be
typed -- described, specified, and refined through keyboard interaction.

**The Founder: Morgan Venable**

Morgan Venable composed this very research digest via voice input to Claude Code
-- directing an AI to aggregate research, synthesize findings, and produce
structured documentation that would traditionally require a research assistant and
technical writer. This is itself a workflow choice: voice for high-level direction
and ideation, with keyboard available for precision when wanted. Different people
will find different balances.

### What This Case Study Reveals

1. **AI makes each work session more consequential.** A single developer's
   direction now drives the output of what was previously a multi-person team.
   Whether that direction comes through typing, voice, or some combination, each
   act of human intent carries more weight.

2. **The work shifts up the abstraction stack.** Instead of writing `for (int i=0;
   i < len; i++)`, the developer expresses "implement the Viable protocol handler
   with VIA command passthrough for macros." The human contribution is cognitively
   denser. This is knowledge transformation, not knowledge telling -- regardless
   of input modality.

3. **The scope of work expands.** When one person operates as an entire team, they
   don't work less -- they work across a broader surface for longer sessions. The
   ergonomic demands on whatever input method they use *increase*. Whether typing,
   speaking, or combining modalities, the human body is engaged for more hours at
   higher cognitive intensity.

4. **The stakes of incapacity multiply.** A developer sidelined by RSI, voice
   strain, or any other input-method injury doesn't lose one person's productivity
   -- they lose an entire team's output. The case for accommodating the human body
   scales with AI force multiplication.

### The Ergonomic Implication

The question isn't "will people type?" -- it's "how will people sustain the
cognitive work that AI amplifies?" For many, that means typing, and it means
typing that doesn't cause pain. For others, it may mean voice, gaze, or
combinations we haven't yet refined. Svalboard addresses the typing case
with the best evidence base available (DataHand clinical research, 71% pain
reduction). But the deeper point is that *any* input method used at this
intensity needs to be sustainable for the human body.

## 13. Multimodal Workflows: The Real Frontier

AI isn't just changing what we write -- it's changing what's *possible* in how we
interact with computers. The most interesting question isn't "keyboard or voice?"
but "what combination of input modalities, augmented by AI understanding, lets
each person do their best work while protecting their body?"

### The Current Landscape of Input Modalities

**Keyboard**: The research in this digest establishes typing as a powerful
cognitive tool -- knowledge transformation through physical composition, with
decades of evidence on automaticity, flow states, and the writing-thinking
connection. For people who can type comfortably, it remains the densest channel
for expressing precise intent.

**Voice**: Tools like Talon Voice (created by Ryan Hileman after developing severe
hand pain in 2017) provide full computer control by voice, scriptable in Python.
Talon integrates speech recognition, noise detection (pop for click, hiss for
drag), and eye tracking into a unified system. Its community has built
`talonhub/community`, a standard command grammar providing comprehensive
desktop control.

**Structural voice editing (Cursorless)**: Created by Pokey Rule (also after
developing RSI), Cursorless is a spoken language for structural code editing in
VS Code. Its "hat" system places colored decorations above every token; users
speak commands referencing these hats to navigate and edit code at the AST
(abstract syntax tree) level. A single utterance like "move argument air to after
drum" replaces what would be multiple keyboard operations. The learning curve is
steep, but experienced users report meeting or exceeding keyboard editing speeds.
~7,000 VS Code installs, 5.0 rating, actively maintained as of 2026.

**Gaze tracking**: Tobii eye trackers integrate with Talon to position the cursor
via gaze, with head tracking for refinement. This isn't gaze-only typing (which
is slow and exhausting) but gaze as a *complement* -- your eyes indicate where,
your voice or hands indicate what. Svalboard's integrated Trackpoint and
trackball pointing already embody this principle of tight coupling between text
input and cursor control; gaze tracking extends it further.

### Where AI Changes Everything

The traditional limitation of voice coding was that you had to learn a rigid
command grammar. You couldn't just *say what you meant* -- you had to translate
your intent into the system's vocabulary. LLMs dissolve this barrier.

**GitHub Copilot Voice** (2022-2024, technical preview) was the first major
attempt to combine LLM code generation with voice in a mainstream IDE. Users
stated intent in natural language; the LLM generated code or navigated the
codebase. The preview ended in 2024, with learnings transferred to VS Code's
Speech extension.

**"Vibe coding" with voice** is the emerging pattern (2025-2026): speech-to-text
tools like Wispr Flow combined with AI code editors like Cursor. Users speak
naturally to describe functionality; the LLM generates code. Humans speak at
150+ WPM versus typing at 40-80 WPM. When an LLM understands your intent,
voice input shifts the bottleneck from mechanical production to conceptual
thinking.

**GazePointAR** (Lee et al., CHI 2024) demonstrated a context-aware multimodal
voice assistant that uses eye gaze + pointing gestures + conversation history
to disambiguate spoken references. Users could ask "what's over there?" while
looking at an object, and the system resolved the referent via computer vision
and an LLM. This points toward a future where AI doesn't just understand your
words but understands *where you're looking and what you're pointing at* when
you say them.

**SpeakFaster** (Google Research + Team Gleason, 2024, *Nature Communications*)
showed that LLMs can amplify reduced-bandwidth input channels: ALS patients using
eye-gaze typing entered only word initials, and the LLM expanded to full phrases
using conversational context. 57% fewer motor actions, 29-60% faster entry rates.
If AI can do this for gaze typing, imagine what it can do for voice + gaze +
keyboard in combination.

### The Cost of Switching Between Modalities

One reason the Svalboard integrates pointing (Trackpoint/trackball) directly into
the keyboard is to eliminate the hand-homing cost between keyboard and mouse.
Research quantifies this cost:

- **Card, English & Burr (1978)**: The foundational study applying Fitts' Law to
  input devices. Every keyboard-to-mouse transition involves a ballistic movement
  plus fine adjustment, plus the cognitive overhead of switching from symbolic
  (keyboard) to spatial (pointing) mode.

- **Gloria Mark (UC Irvine)**: Context switches take an average of 23 minutes to
  fully recover from. Micro-level switching (keyboard to mouse, hundreds of times
  daily) consumes an estimated 45-90 minutes of productive output from the
  accumulated micro-recoveries.

- Svalboard's approach -- fingers never leave their wells, pointing happens
  under the same hands -- eliminates homing entirely. Cursorless achieves the same
  for voice users by eliminating spatial targeting altogether. Both are workflow
  optimizations that AI amplifies: when each cycle of intent-expression is more
  consequential, the cost of interrupted flow is higher.

### The Multimodal Future

The most powerful workflows will likely combine modalities fluidly:

- **Voice** for high-level direction, ideation, and rapid rough-drafting
- **Keyboard** for precise editing, knowledge-transforming composition, and the
  physical-cognitive feedback loop that many people find essential to deep thinking
- **Gaze** for pointing and context -- telling the AI *where* you mean without
  lifting your hands
- **AI** as the interpreter that bridges these modalities, understanding intent
  from the combination of what you say, where you look, and what you type

This isn't speculative -- it's happening now. Talon + Cursorless + eye tracking
is a working multimodal coding environment used daily by developers. The tools
are still rough, the learning curves steep, and the integration imperfect. But
the trajectory is clear.

### What This Means for Ergonomic Design

The question for input device designers isn't "which modality wins?" -- it's
"how do we support all of them?" Svalboard already combines typing and
pointing in a single ergonomic package. The natural extensions are:

- Voice integration that works *alongside* typing, not as a replacement
- Gaze-aware interfaces that reduce pointing demands
- AI that learns the user's multimodal patterns and anticipates intent
- Workflows where the user moves fluidly between modalities based on what
  the task demands and what their body can sustain

The goal isn't to eliminate any modality. It's to give people choices -- and
to make sure the choices they prefer are sustainable for their bodies over a
career.

### Research Pointers

- Voiceye (Paudyal et al., ACM DIS 2020): Multimodal IDE combining voice,
  gaze, and switches for coding. N=29 study demonstrated viability.
- ReType (University of Auckland/Bath, CHI): Gaze-based text correction --

## 14. The Craftsperson Argument

### Intellectual Tradition

- **Crawford (2009)** *Shop Class as Soulcraft*: The hand-mind connection in
  skilled manual work provides meaning and engagement that supervision of automated
  processes does not.

- **Sennett (2008)** *The Craftsman*: Iterative hand-mind connection in skilled
  work produces both better outcomes and deeper satisfaction.

- **Newport (2016, 2019)** *Deep Work / Digital Minimalism*: Focused skilled
  production is both economically valuable and personally satisfying.

### Professional Identity

- Knowledge workers identify with their tools (Barley & Kunda, 2001)
- Keyboard choice and typing skill function as signals of expertise
- The mechanical keyboard community has grown, not shrunk, during the AI era
- The programming-as-craft tradition (Knuth's literate programming, Software
  Craftsmanship Manifesto) emphasizes skilled manual creation

### The Counter-Argument to "AI Will Replace All Typing"

The photography-painting analogy is instructive but must be stated honestly.
Photography *did* kill painting as a mass commercial livelihood. Portrait painters,
miniaturists, and illustrators were devastated within a generation. What survived
was painting as fine art -- a much smaller, more elite market that was freed from
representational obligations to pursue expression and abstraction.

The honest parallel for AI and writing: AI will likely devastate writing as a mass
profession (technical writing, marketing copy, basic journalism, boilerplate code).
A smaller number of human writers will survive doing high-craft, high-judgment work.
But the *cognitive act* of writing -- knowledge transformation through composition
-- remains valuable for the writer themselves, regardless of whether the output has
commercial value. Just as amateur painting persists because it serves the painter,
the act of writing serves the thinker.

## 15. Synthesis: The Question Is How You Want to Work

### Five Things the Research Tells Us

1. **Writing is thinking, not just communication.** The act of composing text
   generates understanding that doesn't exist before the writing happens (Bereiter
   & Scardamalia, Galbraith, Emig, Vygotsky). AI can generate text, but it cannot
   generate the *cognitive transformation in the writer* that occurs during
   composition. Many people experience this transformation most powerfully through
   physical typing. Others find it through longhand, dictation, or conversation.
   The research says the mechanism matters; your experience tells you which
   mechanism works for you.

2. **Fluent input -- whatever form it takes -- enables deeper thinking.** When
   transcription is effortful, it consumes working memory needed for higher-order
   composition (McCutchen, Alves). When it's automatic, the mind is free for
   knowledge transformation. This is true whether "fluent input" means expert
   touch typing (Logan & Crump), practiced voice coding (Cursorless), or any other
   modality that has become second nature. The point is mastery, not modality.

3. **AI is reshaping every workflow.** 46% of new code is AI-generated (GitClear,
   2025). Solo developers operate as entire teams. Writers direct AI to produce
   first drafts, then shape the output. The human role is shifting from production
   to direction, curation, and the knowledge-transforming work that happens
   in the human mind through the act of composition.
   This shift changes *what* we input, not *whether* we input.

4. **The body is the bottleneck, and the stakes are rising.** When AI multiplies
   the output of each human work session, the human's capacity to sustain that
   session becomes the limiting factor. RSI, voice strain, eye fatigue -- any
   breakdown in the input channel shuts down not one person's output but a
   multiplied capability. The case for accommodating the human body scales with
   AI force multiplication.

5. **The future is multimodal and personal.** Voice, gaze, keyboard, pointing,
   and AI-mediated intent recognition will combine in ways we're only beginning
   to explore (Talon, Cursorless, GazePointAR, SpeakFaster). The best workflow
   is the one that lets *you* sustain deep, knowledge-transforming work over a
   career. That will look different for different people, and the research
   supports multiple paths.

### What This Means for the Svalboard

Svalboard exists because many people find that physical typing is their
preferred and most powerful mode of knowledge transformation -- and because
conventional keyboards cause pain that destroys the cognitive benefits of that
preference. The DataHand evidence (71% pain reduction, Harrington study;
productivity maintenance, Fernandez/Stanford) established that the finger-well
paradigm can sustain typing over careers. Svalboard improves on that
foundation (lower force, shorter travel, per-key tunability, integrated pointing,
modern firmware).

But the Svalboard isn't an argument that everyone must type. It's an argument
that if typing is how you think best -- and the research says it is for many
people -- then you deserve hardware that won't take that away from you. In an
AI world where each work session carries more consequence, protecting your
preferred input method is protecting your cognitive capability.

The right question for each person isn't "should I type or speak or look?" It's:
"How do I want to structure my workflow, and what does my body need to sustain it
for the next thirty years?"
