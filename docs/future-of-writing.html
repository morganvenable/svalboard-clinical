The Future of Writing in an AI World

Why the way you interact with your tools matters more than ever, and how to think about your own workflow.

Writing Is Thinking

The most important insight from decades of cognitive science research is that writing is not just a way to record thought. It is a way to generate thought.

Bereiter and Scardamalia (1987) drew a foundational distinction between knowledge telling and knowledge transforming. Knowledge telling is what happens when a writer retrieves content from memory and transcribes it with minimal restructuring. Knowledge transforming is what happens when the act of writing itself creates new understanding through a dialectic between what you know and how you're trying to communicate it. The writer thinks thoughts they would not have thought without writing.

Modern AI systems clearly do something that resembles knowledge transformation: they synthesize novel connections, restructure understanding for different audiences, and generate insights not explicitly present in any single source. The question is not whether AI can transform knowledge in its output. It plainly can.

The question is what happens inside the human during the act of composition. When you write, you are not just producing text. You are reorganizing your own understanding. The cognitive transformation happens in the writer's mind as a consequence of the struggle to articulate. When AI writes for you, the output may be excellent, but you don't undergo that transformation yourself. The understanding remains the AI's, not yours.

The Evidence Base

  • Emig (1977): Writing is unique among language processes because it is both productive and immediately available for review. You read what you wrote, creating a feedback loop absent from speech.
  • Vygotsky (1934/1986): Written language forces the writer to create context that a listener would normally provide, requiring more explicit and structured thinking.
  • Langer & Applebee (1987): Analytic writing led to better content learning than reading alone across large empirical studies.
  • Galbraith (1999, 2009): "Discovery" writers gained more new ideas through writing than high-outline planners, because text production itself generates connections unavailable through planning alone.
  • Kellogg (2008): Extended the framework to three stages: knowledge telling (novice), knowledge transforming (intermediate), knowledge crafting (expert). Reaching the expert stage takes 10+ years of deliberate practice.

Writing and Speech Are Cognitively Different

Writing and speaking are not the same cognitive operation delivered through different output channels. They engage fundamentally different processes.

Dimension Speaking Writing/Typing
Planning Incremental, real-time Recursive, asynchronous
Revision Cannot unsay; can only repair Can delete, reorder, restructure
Memory load Must hold plan in working memory Text persists as external memory
Social context Listener present; co-constructed Writer alone; must construct context
Working memory Phonological loop dominant All three WM components engaged

Speaking is incremental and socially situated (Levelt, 1989). Writing is recursive and self-monitored (Flower & Hayes, 1981). The persistence of the written trace fundamentally changes the cognitive task by offloading memory and enabling revision (Bourdin & Fayol, 1994). Writing recruits visual-spatial working memory components that speaking does not (Kellogg, 2007).

These are not minor differences. They mean that the experience of composing text by writing (typing, handwriting) engages cognitive processes that dictation and speech do not automatically activate. For people who find that writing is how they think best, this matters deeply.

The Neuroscience of Typing

Brain Regions

Typing activates the left superior parietal lobule, left premotor cortex, bilateral supplementary motor area, and cerebellum (Purcell et al., 2011; Planton et al., 2013). Handwriting recruits contralateral primary motor cortex and uniquely engages Exner's area with letter-specific motor traces (Longcamp et al., 2003). Speaking activates Broca's area, primary motor cortex for mouth and larynx, and superior temporal gyrus for auditory feedback (Perrone-Bertolotti et al., 2014).

Expert vs. Novice Typing

A critical gap in the literature: most neuroimaging studies comparing "typing" with handwriting use novice or hunt-and-peck typists. Expert touch typists show different patterns. Novel motor sequences (hunt-and-peck) heavily activate prefrontal and parietal regions; well-learned sequences (touch typing) shift to subcortical SMA and basal ganglia control (Segal & Petrides, 2012). Even imagining typing specific words activates finger-specific motor cortex, demonstrating that typing does create embodied cognitive representations (Ihara et al., 2021).

The "handwriting advantage" found in many studies may largely reflect a comparison between skilled handwriters and unskilled typists, not an inherent modality difference.

The Fluency Paradox

Here is the central tension for understanding input in an AI world:

Too little fluency starves higher-order thinking. When typing (or any input method) is effortful, it consumes working memory needed for composition (McCutchen, 1996). Slow typists pause mid-word for transcription; fast typists pause at clause and sentence boundaries for planning (Alves, Castro & Olive, 2008). Typing fluency shifts cognitive bottlenecks upward to where they belong.

But transcription that is too easy may bypass knowledge transformation entirely. When AI generates text for you, or when dictation flows without the friction of composition, you may skip the very cognitive process that makes writing valuable as a thinking tool.

The sweet spot is where input is automatic enough not to interfere with thinking, but the human is still doing the generative cognitive work. For expert typists, this is touch typing at speed. For practiced voice coders, this may be structural editing via Cursorless. For others, it may be longhand writing, or dictation with active revision. The right answer depends on the person.

Flow and Automaticity

Logan and Crump (2011) proposed that skilled typing has two loops: an outer loop handling language and planning (conscious), and an inner loop handling keystroke execution (automatic). In expert typists, the inner loop is fully automatic: typists cannot report which fingers they use or where keys are located. This frees the outer loop entirely for composition.

Csikszentmihalyi's (1990) flow state requires that the motor act not interrupt the cognitive act. Chenoweth and Hayes (2001) showed that faster typists produced longer uninterrupted bursts of text, consistent with sustained flow. Galbraith (2009) argued that "knowledge-constituting" writing requires sustained, uninterrupted text production: externalizing thought at the speed of ideation.

How AI Is Changing the Work

Productivity Effects

  • Noy & Zhang (2023, Science): ChatGPT reduced writing time by 40% with the largest effect for lower-skilled writers. Early studies suggested AI primarily raised the floor.
  • Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2023, NBER): AI assistance increased customer support productivity by 14% on average, with 34% gains for novice workers.

Important update for 2026: These early studies used 2023-era models on routine tasks. By 2026, with frontier models (Claude Opus 4.5/4.6, GPT-5.2, Gemini 3), AI demonstrably raises the ceiling too. Solo developers using agentic coding tools are doing the work of entire teams, not by lowering the bar, but by amplifying expert capability. The correct framing is: AI raises the floor for novices AND multiplies the throughput of experts, through different mechanisms.

The Cognitive Shift

  • Lee, Liang & Yang (2022, CHI): AI suggestions shifted writers from generation to evaluation/selection mode. More time choosing between options, less time generating from scratch.
  • Ippolito, Yuan, Coenen & Burnam (2022): Professional writers reported AI suggestions sometimes derailed their train of thought and pushed them toward more generic text.
  • Doshi & Hauser (2024, Science Advances): AI-assisted stories were rated higher individually, but the overall corpus was more homogeneous. AI boosts individual quality at the cost of collective diversity.

The Editing Reality

Human writers made substantive edits to 70%+ of AI suggestions (Lee et al., 2022). Longer AI suggestions reduced keystrokes but increased cognitive load; users who accepted long suggestions without editing produced lower-quality text (Bhat, Mehta & Yun, 2023). AI consistently struggles with domain-specific accuracy, consistent voice, logical coherence in complex arguments, and contextual appropriateness.

The Deskilling Risk

Theory

Bainbridge's "Ironies of Automation" (1983) identified the core paradox: the more reliable the automation, the less practice the human gets, and the less competent they become at the very task they must perform when automation fails. Applied to writing: if AI handles 95% of text, the human's ability to handle the remaining 5% degrades.

Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens (2000) documented "complacency" and "out-of-the-loop" effects at higher automation levels. Risko & Gilbert (2016) identified writing as a "generative" cognitive task where offloading carries particularly high risk.

Evidence

  • Dell'Acqua, McFowland, Mollick et al. (2023, Harvard/BCG): The "jagged frontier" study. AI improved performance on tasks inside the AI's capability boundary by 40%, but decreased performance by 19% on tasks outside the boundary when consultants over-relied on AI. The strongest evidence for "falling asleep at the wheel."
  • Darvishi et al. (2024): Students relying heavily on AI-generated text showed weaker unassisted writing performance, especially for argumentative and analytical writing.
  • Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011, Science): People recall less when they know information is digitally accessible. Foundational for cognitive offloading concerns.

The Connection to Knowledge Transformation

If AI handles the knowledge-telling work, humans may lose the capacity for knowledge transformation. Not because the skill was automated, but because the practice that develops it was eliminated. Knowledge transforming is not innate; Kellogg (2008) estimates it takes 10+ years of deliberate writing practice. You cannot maintain a skill you do not exercise.

AI and Programming: The Current State

The Numbers (2025-2026)

  • The vast majority of professional developers now use AI coding tools daily
  • The share of new code that is AI-generated is large and growing rapidly; major tech companies report substantial fractions of their codebases are now AI-authored
  • Anthropic has reported that most of the code for Claude Code is written by Claude Code itself

The METR Study

METR (July 2025) ran a randomized controlled trial with 16 experienced open-source developers on 246 issues using Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet. Developers took 19% longer with AI tools. They predicted AI would make them 24% faster, and even after experiencing the slowdown, believed AI had sped them up by 20%. Less than 44% of AI generations were accepted.

A February 2026 update expanded to 57 developers and 800+ tasks but encountered severe selection bias: 30-50% of developers refused to submit tasks they didn't want to do without AI. METR acknowledged their estimates likely underrepresent actual AI productivity gains.

The METR study matters not as evidence that AI doesn't help, but as evidence that the benefits are hardest to measure in complex, familiar codebases where deep context matters most.

Code Quality Concerns

GitClear (2025) found that refactoring dropped from 25% to less than 10% of code changes, while copy-pasted code rose from 8.3% to 12.3%. CodeRabbit (December 2025) found AI co-authored code had roughly 1.7x more major issues and 2.74x higher security vulnerability rates.

What Changes, What Doesn't

Typing shifts from boilerplate to higher-level direction and prompting. Developers express "what I want" instead of "how to do it." More time goes to reviewing, editing, and rejecting AI output. Total keystrokes may decrease, but per-keystroke cognitive value increases. Manual input remains essential for specifying intent precisely, editing AI output, working in novel domains, and security-critical code.

Case Study: The Super-Powered Developer

Svalboard itself illustrates how AI reshapes knowledge work across multiple roles.

The Developer

A solo firmware and software developer who, with an agentic AI coding subscription, does the work of what would traditionally require a large development team. His contributions span keyboard firmware, web-based configuration tools, protocol implementations, and systems integration, a scope normally demanding specialists across embedded systems, web development, protocol design, and DevOps.

This is the "super-powered developer" pattern of 2025-2026: an experienced engineer using agentic AI as a force multiplier across an entire product stack. The AI handles boilerplate, generates implementations from architectural direction, runs tests, and iterates. But every cycle requires the developer to express intent, make architectural decisions, review code, apply corrections, and shape generated output into production-quality software.

The UX Designer

A UX designer on the same project uses AI tools for rapid prototyping and design iteration on the product's custom web-based layout editor. A designer who can now prototype functional interfaces directly, without waiting for developer cycles. AI generates implementations from design intent, but the intent must still be specified and refined.

The Founder

The founder of the project composed the research underlying this page via voice input to an AI assistant, directing it to aggregate research, synthesize findings, and produce structured documentation that would traditionally require a research assistant and technical writer. Voice for high-level direction and ideation, with keyboard available for precision when wanted. Different people will find different balances.

What This Reveals

AI makes each work session more consequential. One person's direction now drives the output of what was previously a multi-person team, regardless of input modality. The work shifts up the abstraction stack toward knowledge transformation. The scope expands: when one person operates as an entire team, they don't work less; they work across a broader surface for longer sessions, increasing the ergonomic demands on whatever input method they use.

And the stakes of incapacity multiply. A developer sidelined by RSI, voice strain, or any other input-method injury doesn't lose one person's productivity. They lose an entire team's output. The case for accommodating the human body scales directly with AI force multiplication.

Multimodal Workflows: The Real Frontier

AI isn't just changing what we write. It's changing what's possible in how we interact with computers. The most interesting question isn't "keyboard or voice?" but "what combination of input modalities, augmented by AI understanding, lets each person do their best work while protecting their body?"

Voice Coding

Talon Voice, created by Ryan Hileman after developing severe hand pain in 2017, provides full computer control by voice, scriptable in Python. It integrates speech recognition, noise detection (pop for click, hiss for drag), and eye tracking into a unified system.

Cursorless, created by Pokey Rule (also after developing RSI), is a spoken language for structural code editing in VS Code. Its "hat" system places colored decorations above every token; users speak commands referencing these hats to navigate and edit code at the abstract syntax tree level. A single utterance replaces what would be multiple keyboard operations. The learning curve is steep, but experienced users report meeting or exceeding keyboard editing speeds. Roughly 7,000 VS Code installs, 5.0 rating, actively maintained as of 2026.

Gaze-Assisted Input

This is not gaze-only typing (which is slow and exhausting) but gaze as a complement to other modalities: your eyes indicate where, your voice or hands indicate what.

  • GazePointAR (Lee et al., CHI 2024): A context-aware multimodal voice assistant using eye gaze + pointing gestures + conversation history to disambiguate spoken references. Users ask "what's over there?" while looking at an object, and the system resolves the referent via computer vision and an LLM.
  • SpeakFaster (Google Research + Team Gleason, 2024, Nature Communications): An LLM-powered interface for eye-gaze typing users with ALS. Users type only word initials; the LLM expands to full phrases using conversational context. 57% fewer motor actions, 29-60% faster entry rates. If AI can amplify reduced-bandwidth input this dramatically, imagine what it can do for voice + gaze + keyboard in combination.
  • ReType (University of Auckland / University of Bath, CHI): A gaze-based text correction system. You look at a typo, start typing the correction, and the system identifies the target from your gaze. Hands stay on keyboard.

LLM-Enhanced Voice

The traditional limitation of voice coding was rigid command grammars. LLMs dissolve this barrier. "Vibe coding" with voice (2025-2026) combines speech-to-text tools like Wispr Flow with AI code editors. Users speak naturally to describe functionality; the LLM generates code. Humans speak at 150+ WPM versus typing at 40-80 WPM. When an LLM understands your intent, voice input shifts the bottleneck from mechanical production to conceptual thinking.

The Cost of Switching Between Modalities

One reason Svalboard integrates pointing (Trackpoint/trackball) directly into the keyboard is to eliminate the hand-homing cost between keyboard and mouse. Card, English & Burr (1978) applied Fitts' Law to this problem: every keyboard-to-mouse transition involves ballistic movement plus fine adjustment, plus the cognitive overhead of switching from symbolic to spatial mode. Gloria Mark's research at UC Irvine documented that context switches take an average of 23 minutes to fully recover from; micro-level switching accumulates into significant lost productive time.

Cursorless achieves the same elimination of spatial targeting for voice users. Both are workflow optimizations that AI amplifies: when each cycle of intent-expression is more consequential, the cost of interrupted flow is higher.

The Multimodal Future

The most powerful workflows will likely combine modalities fluidly:

  • Voice for high-level direction, ideation, and rapid rough-drafting
  • Keyboard for precise editing, knowledge-transforming composition, and the physical-cognitive feedback loop that many people find essential to deep thinking
  • Gaze for pointing and context, telling the system where you mean without lifting your hands
  • AI as the interpreter that bridges these modalities, understanding intent from the combination of what you say, where you look, and what you type

This isn't speculative. Talon + Cursorless + eye tracking is a working multimodal coding environment used daily by developers. The tools are still rough, the learning curves steep, the integration imperfect. But the trajectory is clear.

The goal isn't to eliminate any modality. It's to give people choices, and to make sure the choices they prefer are sustainable for their bodies over a career.

Pain Destroys Everything

This is where the cognitive science meets the ergonomic argument.

The Mechanism

  1. Input automaticity (whether typing, voice, or any other method) enables flow states and frees working memory for composition
  2. Pain disrupts automaticity by demanding conscious attention
  3. Disrupted automaticity consumes cognitive resources meant for composition
  4. The person loses both the physical and cognitive benefits of their practiced input method

The Evidence

  • Eccleston & Crombez (1999): Pain inherently captures attention, disrupting ongoing cognitive tasks
  • Rempel et al. (2006): Computer users with upper extremity pain showed decreased typing speed and increased errors
  • Marcus et al. (2002): Annual incidence of hand/arm symptoms among computer users: 21%. Once symptoms appeared, typing speed decreased 15-30%
  • Moore et al. (2019): Workers with RSI reported not just physical but cognitive disruption: difficulty concentrating, loss of creative momentum, anxiety about flare-ups
  • Feuerstein et al. (1997): Higher keystroke force associated with greater symptoms. Workers who developed symptoms typed with 2-4x the necessary force

If typing is a cognitive tool, then anything that disrupts typing disrupts thinking. The DataHand evidence (71% pain reduction over 3.3 months, Harrington Arthritis Research Center) and Svalboard's improvements on that design (lower force, shorter travel, per-key tunability, integrated pointing) directly support sustained cognitive performance for people who choose typing as their primary input method.

The Modalities

The rest of this site explores four dimensions of workflow in depth. Each has its own page.

Speech is not a replacement for typing. It is a complementary channel optimized for different cognitive modes: ideation, high-level direction, and rapid rough-drafting. Local tools like Handy provide private, model-switchable recognition. Talon Voice and Cursorless demonstrate that voice can achieve typing-equivalent speeds for structural code editing. LLMs dissolve the old rigid-command-grammar barrier, letting you speak intent rather than dictation.

Gaze tracking turns a natural behavior (looking at what you want to interact with) into an input signal. Tobii hardware provides the gold standard; open-source alternatives like Project Gameface and EyeTrax are emerging. The most exciting near-term possibility: combining imprecise webcam gaze with LLM screen context to resolve ambiguity without dedicated hardware.

Motor Input is where the human body is most directly at risk and where the interaction between physical and cognitive performance is best documented. Svalboard's finger-well design, backed by DataHand's clinical evidence, protects the hand-mind connection over a career. Pain doesn't just hurt; it consumes the cognitive resources that make writing a thinking tool.

Memory and Attention address what happens between focused sessions and what governs the quality of attention within them. Capturing fleeting insights, contextualizing them with AI, and protecting focused work from interruption are three sides of the same design problem.

The Question Is How You Want to Work

Five Things the Research Tells Us

1. Writing is thinking, not just communication. Composing text generates understanding that doesn't exist before the writing happens (Bereiter & Scardamalia, Galbraith, Emig, Vygotsky). Many people experience this transformation most powerfully through physical typing. Others find it through longhand, dictation, or conversation. The research says the mechanism matters; your experience tells you which mechanism works for you.

2. Fluent input enables deeper thinking. When transcription is effortful, it consumes working memory needed for composition (McCutchen, Alves). When it's automatic, the mind is free for knowledge transformation. This is true whether "fluent input" means expert touch typing, practiced voice coding, or any other modality that has become second nature. The point is mastery, not modality.

3. AI is reshaping every workflow. A large and rapidly growing share of new code is AI-generated. Solo developers operate as entire teams. Writers direct AI to produce first drafts, then shape the output. The human role is shifting from production to direction, curation, and the knowledge-transforming work that happens in the human mind through the act of composition.

4. The body is the bottleneck, and the stakes are rising. When AI multiplies the output of each human work session, the human's capacity to sustain that session becomes the limiting factor. RSI, voice strain, eye fatigue: any breakdown in the input channel shuts down not one person's output but a multiplied capability.

5. The future is multimodal and personal. Voice, gaze, keyboard, pointing, and AI-mediated intent recognition will combine in ways we're only beginning to explore. The best workflow is the one that lets you sustain deep, knowledge-transforming work over a career. That will look different for different people, and the research supports multiple paths.

What This Means for Svalboard

Svalboard exists because many people find that physical typing is their preferred and most powerful mode of knowledge transformation, and because conventional keyboards cause pain that destroys the cognitive benefits of that preference. The DataHand evidence (71% pain reduction, productivity maintenance, fatigue resistance) established that the finger-well paradigm can sustain typing over careers. Svalboard improves on that foundation with lower force, shorter travel, per-key tunability, integrated pointing, and modern firmware.

But Svalboard isn't an argument that everyone must type. It's an argument that if typing is how you think best, you deserve hardware that won't take that away from you. In an AI world where each work session carries more consequence, protecting your preferred input method is protecting your cognitive capability.

The right question for each person isn't "should I type or speak or look?" It's: "How do I want to structure my workflow, and what does my body need to sustain it for the next thirty years?"
