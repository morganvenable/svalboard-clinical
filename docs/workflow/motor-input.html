Motor Input

The physical interface between human and computer remains the critical link in every workflow, and the one most likely to fail over a career.

Why Motor Input Matters Most

Speech, gaze, and memory tools are all important parts of a workflow. But motor input occupies a special position: it is the modality where the human body is most directly at risk, where failure is most career-threatening, and where the interaction between physical and cognitive performance is best documented.

The research on writing as thinking (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 1987; Galbraith, 2009; Kellogg, 2008) establishes that for many people, the physical act of typing is not just an output channel. It is a cognitive tool that generates understanding through the act of composition. When motor input fails (through pain, injury, or fatigue), the cognitive benefit disappears along with the physical capability.

This is why Svalboard exists: not as a faster keyboard, but as a keyboard that protects the hand-mind connection over a career.

Svalboard's Design Principles

Fingers Never Leave Their Wells

Five directional keys per finger, activated by movements of a few millimeters. No hand movement, no finger extension, no reaching. The finger rests in a well and presses North, South, East, West, or Center. This is the same architecture DataHand established in 1993, with clinical evidence behind it: 71% pain reduction over 3.3 months (Kaiser & Koeneman, Harrington Arthritis Research Center, presented at RESNA 1994).

Integrated Pointing

Trackpoint and/or trackball built into the same hand position. No homing cost between keyboard and mouse. Card, English & Burr (1978) applied Fitts' Law to this problem: every keyboard-to-mouse transition involves ballistic movement plus fine adjustment, plus the cognitive overhead of switching from symbolic (keyboard) to spatial (pointing) mode. Gloria Mark's research at UC Irvine documented that context switches take an average of 23 minutes to fully recover from. Micro-level switching between keyboard and mouse, repeated hundreds of times daily, accumulates into significant lost productive time.

Svalboard eliminates this entirely. Pointing happens from the same hand position as typing.

Low Force, Direction-Specific Tuning

Most keys activate at approximately 20 grams. North keys (extension) ship at approximately 12 grams to reduce load on the extensor tendons. Force is tunable down to 8-10 grams per key per direction.

The force profile follows an inverse-square curve: a crisp magnetic breakaway, then force drops precipitously. Total mechanical work per keystroke is approximately 90% lower than conventional switches. This is not just lower peak force; it is dramatically less total work because the force does not sustain across the travel distance.

Research by Rempel, Serina, Klinenberg, Martin, Armstrong, Foulke & Natarajan (UC/Michigan) concluded that keying finger forces of 48 grams or less should be preferred to minimize biomechanical loads. Svalboard's default forces are well within this threshold; its minimum forces are a fraction of it.

Extreme Anatomical Customizability

Every key cluster adjusts independently across five continuous axes:

Axis Direction What It Controls
X East-west (lateral) Splay between fingers
Y North-south (toward/away from palm) Finger length differences
Z Up-down (height) Vertical position, finger curl accommodation
Roll Rotation around Y axis Natural resting angle of each finger
Yaw Rotation around Z axis Alignment with each finger's actual movement axis

All five axes are continuously adjustable with no detents or preset positions. The physical structure and angles of the fingers are distinct from the axes on which they actually move. Roll and yaw adjustments accommodate this, matching cluster orientation to actual finger biomechanics rather than assuming a generic geometry.

The palmrest adjusts in X, Y, yaw, and height, with thermoformable PLA that can be heat-shaped to match individual palm contours. Fingertip key petals ship in 14mm, 16mm, and 18mm widths.

Hand Size Range

DataHand's minimum practical fit was approximately 70mm palm width. Svalboard accommodates users with palm widths around 60mm and hand lengths around 140mm, with splay reduction of approximately 10mm (Svalboard Lightly) compared to the original geometry. This extends the finger-well paradigm to users who were effectively excluded from DataHand.

Curled postures are also supported: because cluster position and angle are continuously adjustable, users who find a more curled hand position natural (or who have conditions like contractures or arthritis that prevent full extension) can configure clusters to match their resting posture.

Pain Destroys Everything

The cognitive science of typing establishes that expert typing achieves automaticity (Logan & Crump, 2011), freeing working memory for higher-order composition (McCutchen, 1996) and enabling flow states (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). Pain reverses all of this.

The Mechanism

  1. Input automaticity enables flow states and frees working memory for composition
  2. Pain disrupts automaticity by demanding conscious attention (Eccleston & Crombez, 1999)
  3. Disrupted automaticity consumes cognitive resources meant for composition
  4. The person loses both the physical and cognitive benefits of their practiced input method

The Evidence

  • Rempel et al. (2006): Computer users with upper extremity pain showed decreased typing speed and increased errors
  • Marcus et al. (2002): Annual incidence of hand/arm symptoms among computer users: 21%. Once symptoms appeared, typing speed decreased 15-30%
  • Baker & Cidboy (2006): Even without diagnosed RSI, prolonged typing sessions produced measurable increases in muscle tension and accuracy decreases
  • Feuerstein et al. (1997): Higher keystroke force associated with greater symptoms. Workers who developed symptoms typed with 2-4x the necessary force
  • Moore et al. (2019): Workers with RSI reported not just physical but cognitive disruption: difficulty concentrating, loss of creative momentum, anxiety about flare-ups

The Multiplied Stakes

In an AI world where a single developer's work session drives the output of what was previously a multi-person team, the cost of incapacity multiplies. A developer sidelined by RSI doesn't lose one person's productivity. They lose an entire team's output. The case for protecting motor input scales directly with AI force multiplication.

The Integration Principle

Motor input should integrate with other modalities rather than competing with them.

A keyboard that also handles pointing eliminates one modality switch. A keyboard that works alongside voice (press-to-record with Handy, for instance) and gaze (cursor warped to approximate position before fine trackpoint adjustment) creates a fluid multimodal workspace where the person moves between channels based on what the task demands and what their body can sustain.

The goal is not to optimize any single input channel in isolation. It is to build a workflow where the channels reinforce each other: voice for speed, keyboard for precision, gaze for spatial context, AI for intent interpretation. And to make sure the physical interface at the center of it all does not break the person using it.
